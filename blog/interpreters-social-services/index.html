Interpreters for Social Services: A Practical Guide for UK Councils and Social Workers

Mohammad Kashif Khalid, MCIL 10 November 2026 10 min read
Contents

An interpreter for social services is a trained, impartial professional who lets a social worker and a child, parent or adult speak to each other accurately when they do not share a language. In safeguarding, care assessments and mental health work, that accuracy is not a courtesy. It is how decisions about people's lives are made fairly and lawfully.

Yet in busy teams it is still tempting to ask a teenage son to "help translate", or to rely on a neighbour who speaks a bit of Urdu. This guide explains why that is risky, what the law and statutory guidance expect, and how to book and work with the right interpreter. It is written for social workers, team managers and council commissioners, and draws on our day-to-day work interpreting for legal and social services in Punjabi, Urdu and Hindi.

Why Interpreting Matters in Social Care

According to the 2021 Census, about 880,000 people in England and Wales could not speak English well and a further 161,000 could not speak it at all. The Learning and Work Institute notes that the highest numbers live in London, the North West and the West Midlands.

Many more people speak English well day to day but not well enough to follow a child protection conference, a mental capacity assessment or a conversation about removing a child from their care. Stress, trauma and unfamiliar legal language all reduce what someone can understand in a second language.

Rule of thumb: if the conversation could change where someone lives, who looks after a child, or what care a person receives, offer a professional interpreter, even if the person seems to manage in English.

What the Law and Statutory Guidance Say

There is no single "interpreting act" in England. Instead, several duties together mean councils must make sure people can understand and take part in decisions about them.

SourceWhat it requires
Equality Act 2010, s.149The Public Sector Equality Duty: councils must have "due regard" to eliminating discrimination and advancing equality of opportunity. Language is not itself a protected characteristic, but language barriers are closely linked to race, which includes nationality and national origins.
Children Act 1989, s.22(5)(c)When making decisions about a looked-after child, the council must give due consideration to the child's "cultural and linguistic background", alongside their religion and racial origin.
Care and Support Statutory Guidance (Care Act 2014)Councils should be prepared to adapt how they communicate during assessments, including providing interpreters where needed.
Mental Capacity Act 2005, s.1(3)A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision unless all practicable steps to help them have been taken. For someone who does not speak English, an interpreter is an obvious practicable step.
Mental Health Act 1983 Code of PracticePara 4.6 says using relatives and friends as interpreters is not good practice and should only be used exceptionally. Para 14.116 asks the AMHP to book registered, qualified interpreters with mental health expertise.
Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023Sets the framework for child-centred assessment. Local safeguarding procedures built on it typically say an interpreter should be provided where a child or parent speaks another language.

Why Family Members and Children Should Not Interpret

Local safeguarding procedures across England are consistent on this point. Warwickshire's 2025 briefing warns that family members have emotional attachments and could even be perpetrators of abuse or neglect, and that children in particular should not interpret where there are safeguarding concerns. Hertfordshire's procedures allow family to help arrange appointments, but not to interpret within interviews.

The risks are practical, not theoretical:

  • Filtering. A relative may soften, leave out or change what is said, sometimes to protect the family and sometimes to protect themselves.
  • Silencing. A child or adult is unlikely to disclose abuse through the person who may be causing it, or in front of a relative they depend on.
  • Harm to children. Asking a child to interpret for a parent puts them in an adult role and exposes them to information they should not carry.
  • Accuracy. Untrained bilingual people often do not know legal and social care terms in either language, and may summarise rather than interpret.
  • Accountability. A family member is not bound by a code of conduct, confidentiality rules or a complaints process.

Reviews keep returning to the same lesson. A 2023 learning briefing on Child T, published by a Yorkshire safeguarding partnership, found that not using an interpreter at a first hospital visit limited the assessment and meant clear safety advice could not be given.

Book an Interpreter

When Social Workers Need an Interpreter

Plan for a professional interpreter in any of these situations where the person's first language is not English:

  • Section 47 child protection enquiries and strategy-led home visits
  • Child and family assessments, and initial and review child protection conferences
  • Looked-after child reviews, contact arrangements and placement planning
  • Pre-proceedings and Public Law Outline meetings with parents
  • Care Act needs assessments, carers' assessments and care and support planning
  • Section 42 adult safeguarding enquiries
  • Mental capacity assessments and best interests meetings
  • Mental Health Act assessments led by an AMHP
  • Age assessments and support for unaccompanied asylum-seeking children
  • Any meeting where a decision, agreement or signature is expected

Court hearings are usually different: interpreters for family court are normally booked by the court through national contracts, so check the arrangements with your legal team.

Face-to-Face, Telephone or Video?

Remote interpreting is quick and cheap, but it is not always appropriate. Choose the mode that matches the risk and the person.

ModeBest forAvoid for
Face-to-faceChild protection visits, conferences, MHA and capacity assessments, disclosures, anyone distressed, elderly or with hearing difficultiesVery short admin calls

How to Choose the Right Interpreter

Booking "an Urdu interpreter" is only the start. Check these points every time.

1. The right language and dialect

Ask the family what they speak at home, not just their nationality. Many families of Mirpuri heritage in Birmingham speak Pahari-Pothwari rather than Urdu; many British Bangladeshi families speak Sylheti rather than Standard Bengali; and Somali varies by region. Punjabi is written in two scripts. A mismatch can make an interview unreliable.

2. Qualifications and registration

Look for the Diploma in Public Service Interpreting (DPSI) or an equivalent Level 6 qualification, and registration with the National Register of Public Service Interpreters (NRPSI). Full NRPSI status generally requires a qualification plus around 400 hours of public service interpreting. Membership of CIOL or ITI adds a further code of conduct and complaints route.

3. Vetting and safeguarding

Interpreters working with children or adults at risk should hold an up-to-date enhanced DBS check. NRPSI asks registrants to renew their disclosure every five years unless they use the DBS Update Service. Ask for safeguarding training and proof of professional indemnity insurance.

4. No conflict of interest

Community languages are often spoken by close-knit communities. Ask the interpreter to confirm they do not know the family, and give them the family name in advance so they can decline. This matters most in cases involving forced marriage, so-called honour-based abuse or female genital mutilation.

5. Gender, age and cultural fit

Ask whether the person has a preference for a male or female interpreter, especially for disclosures of abuse or intimate care discussions. The MHA Code also asks for interpreters appropriate in terms of sex, religion or belief, dialect, cultural background and age.

Working With an Interpreter: Before, During and After

Before the visit

  1. Book early and allow roughly twice as long as a meeting in one language.
  2. Give a short pre-brief: purpose of the visit, who will be there, any risks and any terms or documents that will come up.
  3. Agree that the interpreter will interpret everything said, including side comments, and will not give advice or opinions.

During the visit

  • Speak directly to the person, not the interpreter, and use the first person ("How are you feeling?", not "Ask her how she feels").
  • Use short sentences and pause regularly so nothing is lost.
  • Avoid jargon and acronyms such as "CP plan", "PLO" or "s.20" unless you explain them.
  • Check understanding by asking the person to explain the key points back in their own words.
  • Never leave the interpreter alone with the family or child.

After the visit

  • Record the interpreter's name, language and dialect, mode (in person, video or phone) and any communication issues in the case notes.
  • Offer a short debrief. Interpreters hear the same distressing details you do, often without the support structures social workers have.
  • Where possible, book the same interpreter for follow-up visits to build trust and consistency.

Translating Written Documents

Interpreting covers spoken conversations. Written material such as conference invitations, child protection plans, care and support plans, assessment reports and safeguarding letters needs translation. Hertfordshire's safeguarding procedures, for example, say invitations and reports for child protection conferences must be translated into a language or medium the family understands.

For short documents an interpreter can sight-translate (read aloud in the other language) during a visit, but the family should still receive a written copy they can keep. Where literacy is low, an audio recording of the translation can help. Documents that may go before a court, or that families must act on, should be translated by a qualified translator and, where needed, certified.

Buying Interpreting Services as a Council

Most councils buy language services through a framework, a local contract or a mix of both. The main national route is the Language Services framework (RM6302), run by the Government Commercial Agency (the former Crown Commercial Service, renamed on 1 April 2026). It runs until 6 May 2028 and covers translation, transcription, and spoken and sign-language interpreting.

Frameworks are convenient, but large national suppliers do not always have the dialect skills or local knowledge that social care needs. Whichever route you use, build these requirements into your specification:

  • NRPSI registration or DPSI (or equivalent) for spoken-language interpreters in safeguarding and legal settings
  • Enhanced DBS checks and safeguarding training
  • Dialect matching, with the right to request a specific interpreter for continuity
  • Confidentiality, UK GDPR compliance and secure handling of case information
  • Clear minimum booking times, cancellation terms and travel charges
  • Quality monitoring and a simple route for social workers to raise concerns

Councils can also contract directly with local specialist providers for languages with high local demand. Smaller opportunities are advertised on Find a Tender and Contracts Finder.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a family member interpret if the person asks for them?

A person may bring a relative or friend as a supporter, but statutory guidance and local procedures say they should not normally act as the interpreter, and never where there are safeguarding concerns. Offer a professional interpreter and record the person's choice and your reasons.

Do we need an interpreter if the parent speaks some English?

Often, yes. Everyday English is not the same as following a legal or clinical discussion under stress. Many safeguarding procedures say an interpreter should be offered for sensitive conversations even when someone appears reasonably fluent.

What is the difference between an interpreter and an advocate?

An interpreter conveys exactly what each side says, impartially. An advocate supports the person to express their views and speaks up for them. Someone may need both, and one person should not try to do both jobs.

Who pays for the interpreter?

The council or service that needs to communicate with the person normally arranges and pays for the interpreter. Families should not be asked to provide or pay for their own.
