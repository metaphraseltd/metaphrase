Healthcare Translation Services: A Practical Guide for the NHS and Private Providers

Mohammad Kashif Khalid, MCIL Updated 13 November 2026 11 min read
Contents

Healthcare translation services turn medical information into a language a patient fully understands. That covers written translation of letters, leaflets and records, and spoken interpreting in consultations, by phone or by video. For NHS trusts, GP practices and private clinics, this work is part of patient safety, consent and equality duties, not an optional extra.

This guide explains which language services exist, when to use each one, what current NHS guidance expects, how the NHS buys these services, and what to check before you choose a provider. It is written for practice managers, clinicians, commissioners and private healthcare providers.

Why Language Support Matters in Healthcare

In the 2021 Census, 8.9% of people in England and Wales named a language other than English or Welsh as their main language. That is roughly 5 million people. The most common were Polish, Romanian, Punjabi and Urdu. NHS England's 2025 improvement framework notes that around 1 million people in the UK cannot speak English well or at all.

In May 2025, NHS England published an improvement framework for community language translation and interpreting. It says provision is inconsistent across the NHS and links language barriers to:

  • more use of A&E when people cannot access primary care
  • more emergency admissions, often linked to misunderstood medication or treatment plans
  • a higher chance of adverse events in hospital, and the litigation costs that follow

One case in the framework shows how small gaps cause real harm. A child from a Romanian-speaking family was referred for an MRI scan under general anaesthetic. The family had spoken interpreting but no translated letter. They understood the time and place but not that the child had to fast, so the scan was cancelled.

Key point: interpreting the conversation is not enough if the letter, leaflet or discharge summary the patient takes home is still only in English.

Types of Healthcare Language Services

Face-to-face interpreting

A professional interpreter attends in person. This is the best option for complex or sensitive appointments: consent discussions, mental health assessments, breaking bad news, maternity and paediatric care, safeguarding concerns and end-of-life conversations.

Telephone interpreting

An interpreter joins by phone, often within minutes. It suits short, simple contacts such as booking, test results or prescription queries.

Video remote interpreting (VRI)

The interpreter joins by video, so both sides can see faces and gestures. It works well for planned outpatient and follow-up appointments and is widely used for British Sign Language.

Document translation

Written translation of anything a patient needs to read and act on, from appointment letters and leaflets to discharge summaries and consent forms. Patients' overseas medical records can also be translated into English for their NHS notes.

NHS England's primary care guidance notes that an appointment with an interpreter typically takes double the usual time, so book longer slots.

Healthcare Documents That Commonly Need Translation

  • Appointment letters, especially fasting, medication or preparation instructions
  • Patient information leaflets and consent forms
  • Discharge summaries and medication guides
  • GP referral letters, clinic letters and test results
  • Maternity notes and antenatal information
  • Mental health care plans and assessments
  • Vaccination and public health information
  • Complaints procedures and patient rights information
  • Overseas medical records, translated into English for clinical use

Medical translation needs subject knowledge as well as language skills. A mistranslated dose, allergy or fasting instruction can cause harm, so use translators with medical and NHS terminology experience, and have high-risk documents checked by a second linguist.

What NHS Guidance Says

Primary care: NHS England guidance for commissioners (2018)

Integrated care boards (ICBs) are responsible for making sure translation and interpreting are available to patients using primary care. NHS England's guidance for commissioners sets out eight principles. Among them:

  • Patients should not be asked to pay for interpreting or to bring their own interpreter.
  • Patients should be offered a professional interpreter, not rely on family and friends.
  • Spoken-language interpreters should be registered with the National Register of Public Service Interpreters (NRPSI) and hold the Diploma in Public Service Interpreting (Health).
  • Important documents should be translated.

All NHS settings: the 2025 improvement framework

The improvement framework applies to primary care, NHS trusts and ICBs. Its recommendations for trusts include director-level accountability for language services, board-level oversight, and, when procuring a new provider, defining qualification and training standards and checking that interpreters are registered. It also recommends clearer recording of patients' language needs in electronic records.

Family members and children

Professional guidance consistently warns against using relatives as interpreters. Doctors of the World explains why: friends and family may not know medical terms in both languages, may filter what is said, and signs of abuse or neglect can be missed. Children under 16 should not be used.

The law

Under the Public Sector Equality Duty in the Equality Act 2010, NHS bodies must have due regard to eliminating discrimination and advancing equality of opportunity. Language is not itself a protected characteristic, but language barriers are closely linked to race, which includes nationality and national origins. Valid consent and the Mental Capacity Act 2005 also require that patients get information in a form they can understand.

How the NHS Buys Translation and Interpreting

There is no single national NHS supplier. Trusts and ICBs buy language services through framework agreements, their own tenders, or a mix of both. The Association of Translation Companies lists routes including NHS Shared Business Services, Crown Commercial Service (now the Government Commercial Agency), ESPO, NHS Commercial Solutions, Health Trust Europe, YPO and NEPO, as well as direct procurement.

  • NHS SBS Interpretation and Translation Services framework. Awarded in December 2025, with lots for face-to-face spoken language, BSL, telephone, document translation, video interpreting and a one-stop managed service.
  • Language Services framework (RM6302). Run by the Government Commercial Agency for the wider public sector.
  • Direct contracts. Practices, PCNs and smaller providers often contract directly with specialist local suppliers, especially for languages in high local demand.

So "who is the best translation provider for the NHS?" has no single answer. The best provider is the one that meets NHS quality standards for the languages, settings and turnaround your patients need.

How to Choose a Healthcare Language Provider

  • Qualifications. Interpreters with DPSI (Health) or another Level 6 qualification and NRPSI registration; translators who are CIOL or ITI members with medical experience.
  • The right languages and dialects. In Birmingham, for example, many patients of Mirpuri heritage speak Pahari-Pothwari rather than Urdu, and many Bangladeshi families speak Sylheti. Ask what the provider actually covers in-house.
  • Data protection. UK GDPR compliance, ICO registration, secure file transfer and clear deletion policies. For larger NHS contracts, ask whether the supplier has completed the NHS Data Security and Protection Toolkit.
  • Safeguarding. Enhanced DBS checks and safeguarding training for interpreters working with children and adults at risk.
  • Quality checks. A second-linguist review for high-risk documents and a clear route for clinicians to raise concerns.
  • Practicalities. Booking process, minimum booking times, cancellation terms, travel charges and turnaround for urgent work.

Can We Use Google Translate or AI Tools?

Not for clinical decisions. NHS England's framework warned that AI translation apps could affect patient safety. Free tools can get medical terms, doses and negatives wrong, and pasting patient details into them can breach data protection rules. Use machine translation only where a qualified translator checks the output, and never for consent, diagnosis or medication instructions without that review.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best translation provider for the NHS?

There is no single approved supplier. NHS organisations use framework suppliers and direct contracts. Choose a provider whose interpreters are qualified and registered, who covers your patients' languages and dialects, and who handles patient data securely.

How do NHS trusts choose a language services provider?

Usually through a framework such as NHS SBS or the Government Commercial Agency's RM6302, or through their own tender. NHS England's 2025 framework recommends defining qualification and training standards and checking interpreter registration when procuring.

Who provides video remote interpreting for the NHS?

Video interpreting is a separate lot on the NHS SBS framework, so trusts can buy it from approved suppliers. Many local and specialist providers, including Metaphrase, also offer video interpreting directly.

Do patients have to pay for an interpreter?

No. NHS England guidance says patients should not be asked to pay for interpreting or to provide their own interpreter.

Can a family member interpret at a medical appointment?

It should be avoided. Family members may lack medical vocabulary, may filter information and can stop a patient disclosing abuse. Children under 16 should not interpret. Offer a professional interpreter and record the patient's choice.
